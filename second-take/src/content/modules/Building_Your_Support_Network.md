## Introduction

Support is easier to lean on when you already know who is actually there. This lesson is about getting honest with yourself, not about having a long list.

## Content

Most people overestimate how many people they can count on, and underestimate the one or two who would show up without being asked. The person who answers the phone at a hard hour matters more than ten names you rarely speak to. Support also comes from places that are not family: a caseworker who returns your calls, a program that keeps its appointments, someone from a group who has been where you are. Write the real names down, even if the list is short. A short honest list is something you can build on. A long hopeful one is not.

Answer the three prompts below. One name in each is enough.

## Encouragement

Naming even one person is a real start, and this list can grow.

## Introduction

If family is not part of your support, that does not leave you without any. This lesson points you to the places built for exactly this moment.

## Content

Reentry programs exist to help with the first weeks out, and they are not doing you a favor by taking your call. That is the work. A program can help with housing, a job search, transport to appointments, and paperwork you cannot sort out on your own. Faith groups often run meals, rides, and check-ins whether or not you are a member, and you can accept help without signing up for anything. Peer mentors are people who have come home themselves, which means they answer the questions that are hard to ask anyone else. Calling one of these does not commit you to anything. It just opens a door.

## Encouragement

Reaching out to a program is not starting over. It is using what is there.

## Introduction

The hardest part of asking for help is usually the asking, not the help. A short, clear message takes most of the weight out of it.

## Content

People say yes more often to a specific, small ask than to an open-ended one. "Can you pick me up Thursday at two" is easier to answer than "I need help." Being clear also protects you, because a vague ask leaves room for a vague answer and a long wait. Say what you need, say when, and say thank you. Leave out the apology. You are not a burden for needing a ride, a phone call, or a couch for one night, and most people would rather be asked directly than guess. If the answer is no, that is information, not a verdict on you. Ask the next person on your list.

## Activity

Write a short message you would actually send. Keep it to three sentences: what you need, when you need it, and thank you.

## Encouragement

Asking clearly is a skill, and you just practiced it.

## Introduction

A backup plan is not pessimism. It is the difference between a hard day and a crisis.

## Content

Plans fail for ordinary reasons: a phone dies, a ride falls through, someone moves, a program has a waiting list. Having a second name written down means a failed plan costs you an hour instead of a week. Keep both names somewhere that does not depend on a charged battery, like a piece of paper in your wallet. Add 211 to that paper as your third line, since one call reaches housing, food, and transport help in your area no matter which town you are in. Check in with your primary person before you need them, so the first call in months is not also an emergency.
